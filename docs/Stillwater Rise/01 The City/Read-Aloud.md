# Read-Aloud

Boxed text for the key locations. Each block stands alone and is written to be read as is. No named characters, no clues; sensory only. Pick one on arrival, another when they move through, another when they linger. Clues and people are in [[Crime Scene]], [[Session 01]], and the place notes.

---

## The Agency (Merchant Street)

### The stairs
> Three flights over a dentist, and the second landing still smells like the dentist: clove oil and something burned. The stair rail is painted brown over green over brown, and it wobbles where the bracket pulled loose the winter before last. Somebody has taped a card to the third-floor door with the name typed on it, and the tape has yellowed to the color of a filter. Behind the door a telephone is ringing, and it stops before the second ring every time.

### The front room
> A radiator under the window loses a slow argument with the glass. Two desks, one typewriter, a switchboard the size of a suitcase with six cords, and a coat rack that leans. The window looks down on Merchant Street: a delivery truck idling in its own exhaust, a newsstand, a man selling pretzels from a cart with a wheel that doesn't turn. The light in here is the color of weak tea and it comes through blinds that nobody has opened all the way since the blinds were new.

### The back office
> A door with a frosted pane and a name half scraped off it. Inside, filing cabinets on three walls, the fourth wall a corkboard so layered with pinned paper that the cork hasn't been seen in years. A desk with nothing on it but a blotter, a lamp, and an in-tray. The in-tray has one sheet in it. The room is colder than the front room by a few degrees, and the radiator here is off.

## Pier 9

### The approach
> The street runs out of pavement a block before the water and turns to cinders and broken shell. Grain elevator to the right, a rust-streaked tower with a conveyor that hasn't moved since the war; to the left, a chain-link fence with the bottom curled up like a lip. The river is a smell before it's a sight: mud, diesel, something sweet and rotten underneath. Gulls are working something over on the mudbank. Across the water, half lit by a sky the color of dishwater, a billboard the size of a house shows a plaza that doesn't exist yet, painted gold.

### The pier
> Creosote planks, silvered at the edges and black in the middle where feet go. The pier runs forty yards out on pilings furred with weed, and the last ten yards have no rail on the left side, only stubs where one used to be. A sodium lamp on a bent post at the landward end, still burning in daylight, buzzing. The water under the planks is brown and moves in two directions at once: the river going one way, the eddy under the elevator turning slow and heavy the other, with a skin of foam on it the color of old teeth.

### Under the pier
> Down the bank on the canal side, under the planks, the light goes green and the sound goes soft. Mud to the ankle, then rock, then water. The pilings are close enough to touch, cold and slick, with a tidemark of oil a foot above the surface. Someone has been fishing here for years: a milk crate, a coffee can of bait gone to soup, a line tied off to a nail. Across the canal, thirty yards, the far bank is a wall of tin and tarpaulin and cook-smoke, and every so often a face appears in a gap between sheets and goes away again.

## The Flats

### Delancey Street, morning
> Four-story walk-ups the color of wet cardboard, fire escapes zigzagging down their faces with laundry strung between them like flags of a country nobody recognizes. Stoops on both sides, and on every stoop somebody: a woman shelling peas into her apron, two old men with a checkerboard on a fruit crate, a kid with a stick. A bodega with a hand-painted sign in two languages and a radio playing a ballgame through the open door. Halfway down the block, a building with its windows boarded and a paper notice pasted on the boards. The paper has been rained on and the boards have been painted over twice.

### The clearance line
> The street ends at a plywood fence twelve feet high and a hundred yards long, painted white, with a stenciled sign every twenty feet: RENEWAL AUTHORITY, AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL. Over the top of the fence there is nothing. Not a building, not a roof: sky, and a crane, and a mountain of brick with a bathtub sticking out of it. The dust from the other side settles on everything on this side. The stoops nearest the fence are empty. One has a mattress on it.

### Under the Span
> The expressway comes down out of the sky on concrete legs sixty feet tall and stops. Just stops, a hundred feet up, the road deck ending in rebar and air like a diving board for buses. The sound underneath it is wrong, a hum with no source, and the light is a permanent four in the afternoon. Pigeons in the joints. Water running down one leg from somewhere that shouldn't leak. Somebody has painted a name on the concrete at head height and somebody else has painted over it in the Authority's white.

## The Print Shop

### The street door
> A narrow shopfront with the glass painted black to waist height and gold lettering above it flaking: JOB PRINTING. A bell bracket over the door with no bell in it. Through the clear part of the glass, a room of machines under sheets, and a single bulb burning. The smell reaches the sidewalk: ink, hot metal, and something sweet like gum. A stack of newsprint against the inside of the window has gone yellow on the side that faces the street and stayed white on the other.

### The shop floor
> Iron everywhere. A flatbed press the size of a car under a canvas shroud, a hand press, a paper cutter with a blade like a guillotine, and a mimeograph on a table by itself with its drum still faintly purple. Type cases along one wall, hundreds of little drawers, each with a scrap of paper in a brass holder saying what's inside. A roll of butcher paper on a spindle at the counter, pulled out a yard, with writing on it in block capitals that stops mid-line. Every surface has a layer of ink so thin it's just a darkness.

### The rooms upstairs
> Back stairs, steep, with a rope for a rail. A door at the top that doesn't lock so much as stick. Two rooms: a kitchen with one chair and a bottle and a glass on the oilcloth, and a bedroom that is not a bedroom because the bed is against the wall on its side to make room for the filing cabinets. Card drawers, floor to ceiling, labeled by street. A corkboard with a map of the district on it and pins in the map in three colors. The window looks at the fence, and the fence looks back.

## Tin City

### The embankment
> The on-ramp climbs on concrete legs and under it, on the slope of cinders and fill, a town has grown the way rust grows: tin sheeting, tar paper, doors from somewhere else, a bus with no wheels, a piano with no front. Cook fires in oil drums. Lines of washing. Paths worn into the slope by feet, all of them leading to one wide place in the middle where the ground has been swept. It is warmer down here than on the street, and quieter, and every so often a truck goes over on the ramp and the whole town rings like a struck pan.

### The court
> A sprung armchair on a wooden pallet, under a tarp stretched between two concrete legs. A coffee can on a crate beside it. Ring of oil drums, some burning, some seats. Forty people, or close to it, and every one of them has turned to look and not one of them has stood up. Children under a table. A dog that doesn't bark. On the concrete leg behind the chair someone has chalked rows of tally marks, hundreds of them, in groups of five, going up higher than a person could reach without a ladder.

## The Half Note

### The street
> A basement door under a barbershop, three steps down, with a sign the size of a shoebox that says HALF NOTE in a hand-cut script and a light over it that is red on purpose. Piano through the door, and a bass, and glasses. The alley alongside runs to a stage door propped open with a brick, and the alley smells like beer kegs and cat and the river, which is two blocks off and everywhere.

### The room
> Low ceiling, pressed tin, gone the color of a smoker's thumb. A bar along the right wall with a mirror behind it that's lost its silver in patches, so that the room looks back at itself with holes in it. Twelve tables, red candles in jars, half of them lit. A stand at the far end the size of a kitchen table, with a piano, a drum kit with a cracked cymbal, an upright bass leaning in the corner, and one empty chair with a music stand in front of it. The band plays standards and doesn't look up. Over the bar, a photograph of the room from a better decade, with more people in it.

## The Precinct

### The desk
> Green paint to shoulder height, cream above, both older than anyone in the room. A counter with a brass rail worn bright in two places where elbows go. Behind it, a sergeant on a stool and a board of hooks with keys on it, and behind that a room of desks where typewriters are going and nobody is looking up. The bench along the wall has a drunk on it, asleep, and a woman with her purse on her lap, awake. A ceiling fan turns slowly enough to count the blades. It smells of floor wax, cigarettes, and the coffee that has been on the burner since the shift began.

## The Exchange

### The switchboard floor
> A room the length of a train car, and the whole far wall is a board: a thousand jacks, a thousand little lamps, and a row of women in headsets pulling cords and plugging cords with their hands moving faster than their faces. The sound is a tide. Forty conversations at once, none of them loud enough to hear, all of them together making a noise like rain on a roof. A clock the size of a wagon wheel over the board. A supervisor in a cardigan walking the row with her hands behind her back. At the end of the row, a door marked LINE MAINTENANCE, and it's shut.

### Upstairs
> A corridor of bare bulbs and painted pipe, and at the end of it a door with three locks. Beyond the door the air is warm and dry and smells of oxide and warm tubes. Racks of tape reels floor to ceiling, hundreds, each box labeled in a tiny even hand with a date and a length. A desk with a reel-to-reel on it, turning. One window, and through the window, filling it, the unfinished expressway ending in the sky like a sentence somebody stopped saying.

## Little China

### The hill
> The street climbs away from the river in steps, and the Flats fall away behind. Signs in two alphabets, then mostly one. A butcher with ducks in the window. A herbalist with jars. Old men at a folding table playing a game with tiles that click. The air changes halfway up: less river, more ginger and charcoal and steam from somewhere. At the top of the hill, a gate painted red and gold, the paint fresh, and behind it a roof of new green tile, the only new roof for a mile in any direction.

### The temple hall
> Cool inside, and dim, and the floor is stone worn into shallow dishes where feet have gone for sixty years. Three statues at the far end, gilt gone soft, incense in front of them in a bowl of sand, smoke going straight up because there is no draft. An old man with a broom, sweeping a floor that's already clean. The sound of the broom is the only sound. Overhead, the beams are new wood, pale, and the tiles above them are new, and the light that comes through the one high window falls on the old floor like it's not sure it belongs.

## The Renewal Authority

### The Model Room
> A room like a chapel, high and white, with the blinds drawn and every lamp aimed at the table in the middle. The table is the City. Thirty feet long, every street, every block, in pale wood and glass, and on the low side by the river a plaza in gold leaf where the Flats should be, with a fountain, and an expressway that arrives complete on golden legs. Tiny figures on the plaza, hundreds of them, all walking somewhere. A brass plate on the table edge with the date of a ribbon-cutting engraved on it. The room smells of floor polish and money, and it is very quiet.

## The Weaver Building

### The lobby
> Marble the color of cream, and a floor so polished the ceiling is in it. A wall of brass elevator doors, each with a floor indicator ticking. Above the doors, in letters a foot high, the names of a newspaper, a radio station, and a television channel, stacked. A newsstand in the corner selling the newspaper. A receptionist behind a curved desk with three telephones, all of them ringing softly, none of them being answered until she chooses. High overhead, a skylight, and through it, at the very top of the building, something green: the edge of a garden, and a thread of red silk moving in the wind.
